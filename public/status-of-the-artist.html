⚖️ Status of the Artist and where Canada produces

A plain-language guide for Editors and other Post-Production freelancers. Researched from public sources, listed at the bottom. This is information, not legal or tax advice.

What "status of the artist" means

It is ordinary legislation, not part of the Constitution. Its main job is to let unions, guilds and professional associations be formally recognized so they can bargain collectively for self-employed artists, who normally cannot bargain as a group the way employees do.

Federal

The federal Status of the Artist Act (1992) covers self-employed artists and the people who contribute to their work, but only when they work for federally regulated producers such as federal departments and broadcasters. It does not apply to employees. A certified association gets the exclusive right to bargain for its sector and can negotiate scale agreements. The Canada Industrial Relations Board handles certification.

Québec

Québec passed Canada's first law in 1987, for performing, recording and film artists, and a second in 1988 for visual arts, crafts and literature. In 2022, Bill 35 merged the two. Those laws treat covered artists as independent contractors and give recognized associations bargaining rights. A brief to a House of Commons committee says the federal act and Québec's act are the only laws in the country that create a true bargaining system for artists. The union AQTIS 514 IATSE represents about 8,000 freelancers in more than 200 audiovisual trades, including Post-Production.

Other provinces

Saskatchewan passed a broad act in 2002 that works more as a statement of principles. Other provinces have studied the issue or passed recognition-style laws, but none of them has Québec's bargaining system.

The federal Act, in plain terms

Straight from the Act and the Canada Industrial Relations Board (CIRB) page that explains it, summarized in our words:

  • Purpose. Part II sets up a framework for professional relations between artists and producers that guarantees their freedom of association, recognizes the importance of their contributions to the cultural life of Canada, and protects their rights. (the Act)
  • Who "producers" are. Federal government institutions and the broadcasting undertakings the Act describes, plus associations of producers. The Act does not cover employees of those bodies. (the Act; CIRB)
  • Scale agreement. A written agreement between a producer and an artists' association on minimum terms and conditions for the artists' services. (the Act)
  • Certification. The CIRB recognizes an association as the exclusive bargaining agent for a "sector" of self-employed artists. It lasts three years and renews automatically unless someone files to revoke it or a rival association applies for the same sector. (CIRB)
  • Who decides. The Act's former Tribunal was repealed in 2012, and the CIRB now handles certification. (the Act)

Québec: what Bill 35 changed

Québec's first status law dates from 1987. On June 3, 2022, Bill 35 merged it with the 1988 law for visual arts, crafts and literature. The combined law is now titled the Loi sur le statut professionnel des artistes des arts visuels, du cinéma, du disque, de la littérature, des métiers d'art et de la scène. According to RBD Avocats, a Québec labour-law firm, the main changes were these:

  • Collective bargaining now reaches visual arts, crafts and literature too.
  • Artists have a right to a workplace free of psychological and sexual harassment, and producers must have a prevention and complaints policy.
  • Recognized associations have a duty of fair representation toward the artists they represent, whether or not those artists are members.
  • Interfering with an association, or pressuring artists to join or leave one, is prohibited, with complaints going to the Tribunal administratif du travail (TAT).

The firm also notes that a collective agreement under the law sets only minimum conditions: an artist can still negotiate better terms individually. Sources: RBD Avocats, Feb. 16, 2023 (in French) and the Québec government's announcement of the bill, April 27, 2022 (in French).

A real agreement that names Editors

The AQTIS 514 IATSE and Association québécoise de la production médiatique (AQPM) collective agreement for cinema, 2024 to 2028, recognizes AQTIS 514 IATSE as the sole bargaining agent for the artists covered by its recognitions in the cinema sector. Its pay tables include a "Département du montage" (editing department) that lists Monteur (Editor), Monteur sonore (sound Editor), Assistant monteur, Assistant monteur sonore, Coloriste and Mixeur sonore. The tables show several rate tiers; we have not worked out which applies to which budget, so we do not print rates here. Read it yourself: AQPM, administrative version of June 6, 2025 (in French).

Published stories worth reading

AQTIS 514 IATSE signs a four-year deal with Netflix

On August 26, 2026, AQTIS 514 IATSE announced its first term agreement with Netflix in Québec. It covers four years, so it does not need renegotiating for each Netflix project shot in the province. Broadcast Dialogue describes AQTIS as representing 8,000 freelance professionals in more than 200 roles across design, planning, production and Post-Production. News release (CNW) · Broadcast Dialogue, Aug. 27, 2026

CMPA and DGC ratify a new Standard Agreement

On December 16, 2025, the Canadian Media Producers Association and the Directors Guild of Canada announced a new three-year Standard Agreement covering terms, conditions and rates for directors and other professional crew in film, television and digital media. The DGC describes itself there as representing more than 7,000 people in direction, design, production and editing. CMPA news release

What screen performers told the Canada Media Fund

These are performers, not Editors, but they show how freelance screen work looks across the sector. In October 2026 the CMF, working with ACTRA and the UDA, published Shifting the Lens, a survey by Abacus Data of nearly 1,000 on-screen performers. It found that 69% earned less than $20,000 from on-screen work in 2024, even though nearly seven in ten had worked as performers for more than a decade. Cost was the barrier to training and mentorship cited most often (56%). Four in ten reported harassment, discrimination or microaggressions on a set or at an audition since 2021. CMF, Shifting the Lens (the survey describes performers' own reports, not a count of all performers in Canada).

Parliament looked at the Act and asked for a review

The House of Commons Heritage Committee studied the Status of the Artist Act in the 44th Parliament, hearing 29 witnesses and receiving five briefs. Its first recommendation was an in-depth review of the Act and the Copyright Act. Its second was to add a way to reach a first scale agreement through bargaining and arbitration. A minority report from the NDP quotes the DGC's National Executive Director saying a full negotiation can take years, and that the DGC wants binding arbitration for a first contract, as in Québec. In its September 2023 response, the Department of Canadian Heritage said a review of the Act was not being considered at that time. Committee report · NDP supplementary report · Government response

What this means for an Editor

  • In Québec, the law underpins collective agreements for freelance technicians. The AQTIS 514 IATSE cinema agreement lists Monteur, Monteur sonore and Assistant monteur among its editing-department functions, with set pay tables.
  • Everywhere else, an Editor's pay and conditions come from union agreements and individual contracts, not from a status law.
  • Federally, the Act matters to self-employed artists working for federally regulated producers such as broadcasters, and only where the CIRB has certified their sector. The DGC and AQTIS are both on the federal register.

What we could not confirm

We have not read the certification orders that describe each federal sector, so we cannot say whether picture Editors fall inside the DGC's or AQTIS's federal certification. We also cannot say how the Québec recognitions apply to a particular Editor's contract. Before you rely on any of this, check with AQTIS (Québec), your union local, or the CIRB's certification register. We are also not certain of the details of every provincial act.
