Status of the Artist, tax credits and where Canada produces

A plain-language guide for editors and other post-production freelancers. Researched from public sources, listed at the bottom. This is information, not legal or tax advice.

What "status of the artist" means

It is ordinary legislation, not part of the Constitution. Its main job is to let unions, guilds and professional associations be formally recognized so they can bargain collectively for self-employed artists, who normally cannot bargain as a group the way employees do.

Federal

The federal Status of the Artist Act (1992) covers self-employed artists and the people who contribute to their work, but only when they work for federally regulated producers such as federal departments and broadcasters. It does not apply to employees. A certified association gets the exclusive right to bargain for its sector and can negotiate scale agreements. The Canada Industrial Relations Board handles certification.

Québec

Québec passed Canada's first law in 1987, for performing, recording and film artists, and a second in 1988 for visual arts, crafts and literature. In 2022, Bill 35 merged the two. Those laws treat covered artists as independent contractors and give recognized associations bargaining rights. A brief to a House of Commons committee says the federal act and Québec's act are the only laws in the country that create a true bargaining system for artists. The union AQTIS 514 IATSE represents about 8,000 freelancers in more than 200 audiovisual trades, including post-production.

Other provinces

Saskatchewan passed a broad act in 2002 that works more as a statement of principles. Other provinces have studied the issue or passed recognition-style laws, but none of them has Québec's bargaining system.

What this means for an editor

  • In Québec, the law is the foundation for collective agreements covering freelance technicians, and post-production trades fall under AQTIS's coverage.
  • Everywhere else, an editor's pay and conditions come from union agreements and individual contracts, not from a status law.
  • Federally, the act could matter for a self-employed editor working for a broadcaster, but only if the editor's sector is certified.

What I could not confirm

I could not confirm that picture editors are named in Québec's law or covered by a federal certification. Before you rely on any of this, check with AQTIS (Québec), your union local, or the Canada Industrial Relations Board's certification register. I'm also not certain of the details of every provincial act.

Production tax credits, federal and by province

These credits go to production companies, not to individual editors. They matter because they decide where work lands, and some target post directly: BC's DAVE credit covers post-production and VFX labour, and Saskatchewan adds a bonus for post done in-province.
