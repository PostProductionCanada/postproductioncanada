Ask someone what a film editor does and they can guess. Ask about the music editor and you often get a blank look. The Hollywood Reporter called the job crucial to scoring but little understood outside studio control rooms and dub stages. Berklee describes it as part sound editor, part project manager and part musician. Here is what that adds up to, and how it helps a story along.

## It starts before the composer does

On many films the music editor starts while the picture is still being cut. According to the career guide Reel Canada draws on, they work with the director to decide what the music is for, find a style that suits the story, and mark where music is needed. That marking is called spotting.

Then comes the temp score. A music editor, in one working editor's account, helps build a temporary soundtrack from existing music so everyone can feel the emotional shape of a scene and its pacing. The temp is also what the picture editor cuts to, which means it shapes the rhythm of the film before a single note of the real score exists. A composer's first conversations with the director are often about what the temp is doing in a scene, and what the score should do in its place.

## Deciding where silence goes

The spotting session is where the director, composer, editor and music editor watch the cut together and decide where each cue starts and stops, how music moves between scenes, and where themes return. The music editor writes up the notes with exact timecodes. One editor describes silence as sometimes just as powerful as a cue, and says those moments need to be found as deliberately as the cues. Choosing where music does not play is part of how music moves a story.

## Keeping up with a cut that never stops changing

Picture edits keep changing, and the music has to follow. The music editor cuts and conforms music so it still lands on the right frame, edits licensed songs to fit a scene, and prepares click tracks and session files so the musicians can record in sync. They attend scoring sessions and help with revisions. Reel Canada's guide says they often act as a bridge between the sound and picture teams.

## On the dub stage

In the final mix the music editor works with the re-recording mixers so the score plays properly against dialogue and effects, and, per Berklee, represents the composer's interests in the dubbing sessions. They also prepare the cue sheet, the detailed list of every piece of music in the film, which is what lets royalties be paid when it screens.
