How to reset your mind: sound off, eyes up, step away

Post Production Canada · 2026-11-19

By the third hour on the same scene, you stop seeing it. You know every line, every cut, every breath. Editors have a handful of tricks for getting that first-time feeling back, and the oldest is also the simplest: turn the sound off.

What the silent pass shows you

Film Editing Pro calls turning off the audio and watching only the picture the granddaddy of the review hacks, a callback to silent movies, when the visuals had to carry everything. It suggests going further by closing the timeline and bins and watching the cut full screen, so you see it more as an audience would and less as a list of cut points.

PremiumBeat says the trick is favoured by quite a few Editors, naming Walter Murch and Joe Walker as regular users. Walker, speaking about cutting Denis Villeneuve's films, said he and his team started cutting with the speakers off on Sicario, sometimes treating the film as a silent movie. If the story works on the visuals alone, he said, it makes you very tough on the story. PremiumBeat adds that the habit reinforces "show, don't tell", and often reveals where dialogue, exposition or waffle can be trimmed.

Film Editing Pro also quotes Hitchcock's view of the same idea: a good cut should leave the audience with a clear idea of what's going on even if the sound dropped out.

What to look for

My suggestions, not rules:

  • Can you follow who wants what? If you can't tell without the dialogue, the picture may be leaning too hard on words.
  • Where does it drag? Without the soundtrack propping up a scene, slow stretches stand out.
  • Where does the emotion live? Faces, hands, eyelines and held shots carry it when nothing else can.
  • What changes when the sound comes back? The difference is what sound and music are actually adding. That is useful to Editors, sound Editors and music Editors alike.

Other ways to get fresh eyes

Film Editing Pro lists several: switch the order of shots in a moment that has gone stale, make the cut black and white so colour stops distracting you, or flip it around and listen to the sound with the picture off to hear the story's construction. PremiumBeat points out that watching with someone else, even silently, changes how you see your own work, and that Editors at test screenings often watch the audience rather than the screen.

Why stepping away works, and what the research says

The attention side is well studied. A 2025 paper in Frontiers in Psychology describes a vigilance decrement: performance falls across long tasks, and shorter, more regular breaks offset some of that. It cites earlier work from the University of Illinois showing that brief task switches during a long, repetitive task can help maintain attention. A 2022 meta-analysis in PLOS ONE of breaks of ten minutes or less pooled 22 samples and over 2,300 people and found they boosted vigor and cut fatigue. Summaries of that review say the effect on performance was smaller and varied with the task, and that active breaks like stretching or walking helped more than passive rest. I read summaries of those findings, not the full tables, so treat the details as a guide.

Your eyes need the break too

Canada's Centre for Occupational Health and Safety recommends the 20-20-20 rule for screen work: at least every 20 minutes, take a 20-second break and look at something about 6 metres (20 feet) away. It also suggests blinking several times and looking around. The Canadian Association of Optometrists reminds people to give their eyes regular breaks from screens. The American Optometric Association adds that after two hours of continuous computer use, a 15-minute rest is advisable.

A simple reset routine

1. Do a sound-off, full-screen pass before you change anything. 2. Every 20 minutes, look across the room for 20 seconds. 3. Every hour or two, stand up, move and look at something that isn't a screen. 4. When a scene goes stale, change one thing: black and white, a different order, someone else watching. 5. When you come back, watch it once with sound and write down the first thing that bothers you.
